How Dangerous Is Black Mold? A Feasterville Trevose, PA Guide

What the label means, what a restoration crew can and cannot tell you, and where health questions belong.

By the Feasterville Trevose Water Damage Pros team

24/7 dispatch
(445) 837-0074
☎ Call Now

Black mold is a popular label for dark mold growth, and it carries a lot of fear. Color alone does not tell you what kind of mold you have, and a restoration company is not the right place to ask about health effects. This guide covers what is known about mold and moisture and what to do next.

Any health question belongs with a physician. What a restoration crew can address is the water that lets mold grow and the physical removal of affected materials.

What people mean by black mold

The phrase is often used for any dark green or black growth on walls, ceilings or wood. Many kinds of mold look dark, and color is not a reliable way to identify one. Identification takes laboratory analysis, and the result does not change the basic steps of fixing moisture and removing affected material.

The EPA states that if visible mold is present, sampling is usually unnecessary. The first priority is to find and correct the water problem.

Mold needs moisture

Mold spores are present in indoor air almost everywhere. They need moisture to grow. The EPA calls moisture control the key to mold control and advises drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours. It also suggests keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent.

  • Fix the leak or condensation that supplies the moisture.
  • Dry wet materials quickly.
  • Keep humidity in range with ventilation and dehumidification.
  • Remove porous materials that stayed wet and cannot be cleaned.

What a restoration company can and cannot say

A crew can measure moisture, find affected materials and remove them following the ANSI/IICRC S520 standard for mold remediation. A crew cannot diagnose a health condition or tell you what mold is doing to anyone in your household.

If someone in your home has symptoms or a medical condition, talk with a physician. Be wary of anyone who makes health promises about mold, or promises that it will never return.

Cleaning small areas and when to stop

The EPA says people can usually handle a moldy area of less than about 10 square feet, roughly a 3 foot by 3 foot patch. Larger growth or significant water damage calls for the approach used in commercial buildings. On hard surfaces, scrubbing with detergent and water and drying completely is the usual method.

SituationTypical step
Small spot on a hard surfaceFix moisture, scrub with detergent and water, dry
Spots on drywall or ceiling tilePorous material is usually removed, not cleaned
Large area or repeated growthProfessional assessment and containment
Growth after a sewage or flood eventTreat as contaminated and call for help

Wear gloves, goggles and an N-95 respirator, and never mix bleach with ammonia-containing cleaners. The EPA does not recommend bleach as a routine method.

Local note: where moisture comes from in this area

Heavy rain here can raise groundwater around foundations. The National Weather Service tracks Neshaminy Creek at a gauge near Langhorne, where the record crest on its list is 22.84 feet from 1955, and the 2011 crest was 19.56 feet. Past high water is a reminder that basements and crawl spaces in the area can stay damp for long periods.

If your lowest level stays damp after storms, treat that as the real mold risk. Check the FEMA flood map for your address and ask your insurer what water backup or flood coverage you carry.

How professionals handle mold

Remediation under S520 starts with fixing the water, then containing the work area so spores are not spread. Crews use negative air machines with HEPA filtration, remove affected porous materials and clean the remaining surfaces. Clearance testing afterward can show whether the area was cleaned adequately.

See the mold remediation page for the process in order. Ask whether your state licenses mold work, and ask any company whether its crews follow S520.

Common myths worth dropping

  • Myth: color tells you how serious the mold is. Fact: color does not identify a type.
  • Myth: a test must be done before cleanup. Fact: the EPA says sampling is usually unnecessary when mold is visible.
  • Myth: bleach solves it. Fact: the EPA does not recommend bleach as a routine method.
  • Myth: once cleaned, it is gone for good. Fact: it can return if moisture returns.

Dropping these myths saves money and prevents the wrong fix, which is usually repainting a wall that is still wet.

Questions to take to a physician

If anyone in the home is concerned about health, a physician is the right person to ask. Bring a short note describing where growth was found, how long the area was wet and what was done about it.

A restoration company can supply moisture readings, photos and the scope of cleaning, which may help the conversation. It cannot say what any exposure means for a person.

Meanwhile, the steps that fit every situation are the same: stop the water, dry the area and remove affected porous materials.

What not to do

  • Do not paint or caulk over mold before it is cleaned and dried.
  • Do not disturb a large patch with dry brushing or sanding.
  • Do not run a fan across a moldy surface toward the rest of the house.
  • Do not ignore the source of the water.

Questions from Feasterville Trevose homeowners

Is black mold always dangerous?

Color does not tell you what kind of mold it is, and health questions belong with a physician. The practical steps are the same: fix the moisture and remove affected material.

Should I test the mold in my Feasterville Trevose home?

The EPA says sampling is usually unnecessary when mold is visible. Testing is sometimes used afterward to confirm cleaning, so ask a professional.

Can bleach kill black mold?

The EPA does not recommend bleach as a routine method. Dead mold must still be physically removed, and moisture must be fixed.

How fast does mold grow after a leak?

The EPA advises drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours to prevent growth, so speed matters.
