Emergency storm water damage cleanup in Feasterville Trevose, PA handles water that enters a building during a storm through the roof, windows, siding or doors. Wind lifts shingles and drives rain into gaps, and the water soaks attics, ceilings and walls. This is different from rising floodwater, which is on the flood damage page.

A simple roof leak with no storm event is covered by the ceiling water damage page. This page covers the cleanup after storm events.

When storm water damage is an emergency

Call the 24/7 line when water is entering during or after a storm. Same-day response is offered when crews are available. Storms often damage many homes at once, so early calls help.

Storm safety

  1. Stay away from downed power lines and call 911 and your utility about them.
  2. Keep out from under sagging ceilings.
  3. Turn off power to wet areas only if you can reach the panel while dry.
  4. Cover openings with a tarp only if it is safe, and never climb onto a wet roof.
  5. Photograph all damage before temporary repairs.

How storm water gets in

Lifted or missing shingles

Wind exposes underlayment and rain enters.

Flashing failures

Chimney, vent and wall flashing leak in wind-driven rain.

Window and door gaps

Rain is pushed through seals under pressure.

Gutter and downspout overflow

Water backs up at the roof edge and enters the soffit.

Storm water is outdoor water, so it can carry dirt and debris. Insulation and drywall that soak it are usually removed rather than dried.

Recent flood context

Neshaminy Creek reached 19.56 feet on its gauge near Langhorne in 2011, according to National Weather Service records, and 21.91 feet in 1999. Rain-driven events can bring both roof leaks and rising water. Check your FEMA flood map and talk to your insurance agent about which losses are covered.

Cleanup and repair

Crews extract standing water, then open wet ceilings and walls where needed. Moisture meters and thermal imaging show hidden wetness in attic insulation and wall cavities. Tarps, boarding and temporary repairs protect the building. Roofers and window contractors repair the entry points.

Drying follows, and the structural drying page explains the process. The Pennsylvania Insurance Department advises making temporary repairs to prevent further damage and keeping receipts. Do not make permanent repairs until your insurer approves them.

Safety steps after a storm

Storm damage often comes with downed lines, wet insulation and weakened ceilings. Safety comes before photographs.

  1. Stay away from downed lines and call your utility or 911.
  2. Stay out of rooms with sagging ceilings.
  3. Do not use electrical equipment standing in water.
  4. Document damage from a safe distance once the area is secure.

Documenting storm damage for your insurer

The Pennsylvania Insurance Department advises taking photos or video of damage before cleanup and repairs, and contacting your insurer promptly. Storm claims move in volume, so organized records help.

  • Wide and close photos of every wet or damaged room
  • Photos of the roof or exterior from the ground, taken safely
  • A list of damaged belongings with approximate age and value
  • Receipts for tarps, boarding, hotel stays and equipment
  • Names, dates and notes from every call with your insurer

We add moisture readings and a written scope of work. Your insurer decides coverage, and permanent repairs wait for its approval.

After the storm passes

Once the rain stops, the work shifts from emergency to recovery. Check the attic with a flashlight from the access hatch for wet insulation and daylight where it should not be. Look at ceilings for new stains, and check windowsills and door thresholds for water marks.

Small leaks that go unnoticed after a storm can run during every later rain. A short walk through the house after each major storm catches them early, and a moisture reading shows whether a stain is still active.

Should I tarp the roof myself?

Only if it is safe and you can do it from the ground or a ladder without climbing a wet roof. Otherwise call a professional.

Is storm water clean?

Not necessarily. It can carry dirt and debris, so wet insulation and drywall are usually removed.
