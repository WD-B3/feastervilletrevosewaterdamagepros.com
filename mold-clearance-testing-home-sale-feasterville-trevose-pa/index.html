Mold Clearance Testing for a Home Sale in Feasterville Trevose, PA

What clearance testing shows, when it is requested and how it fits the selling process.

By the Feasterville Trevose Water Damage Pros team

24/7 dispatch
(445) 837-0074
☎ Call Now

A home inspection finds staining, a seller says the leak was fixed years ago and the buyer asks for proof. That is where mold clearance testing comes up. Clearance testing is done after cleaning to check whether the area was cleaned adequately, and it is separate from the cleaning itself.

This guide explains what clearance testing is, who does it and how it fits into a sale in Pennsylvania. It is general information and not legal advice, so ask your agent or attorney about your specific transaction.

What clearance testing is

After a mold cleanup, a third party may collect air or surface samples and send them to a laboratory. The results are compared with conditions in an unaffected area or with the cleanup goals set before work began. The idea is to confirm that the cleaning worked, not to find new problems.

The EPA notes that there are no federal limits for mold in buildings, so sampling cannot show that a building meets a federal standard. It also notes that surface sampling may help confirm that an area was adequately cleaned.

Why buyers and lenders ask for it

Buyers want evidence that a past problem was handled. Lenders and insurers sometimes ask too, especially after a known water loss. A clearance report, together with moisture readings and photos of the repair, gives them a package to review.

  • Moisture readings showing the structure is dry.
  • Photos of the affected area before and after cleaning.
  • The scope of work and the standard followed.
  • Third-party clearance results, if requested.

None of these guarantees anything about the future. They document the state of the home on the day it was checked.

Who should do the testing

The cleaner should not grade its own work when a sale depends on the result. An independent tester has no stake in passing the job. Ask whether your state licenses mold work and whether the tester or the remediator follows ANSI/IICRC S520, the published standard for mold remediation.

Local note: what Pennsylvania sellers must disclose

Pennsylvania's Real Estate Seller Disclosure Law, in Title 68 of the state code, requires a seller to disclose any material defects known to the seller. The seller completes a written statement and gives a signed copy to the buyer before the agreement is signed.

The statute lists subjects the form covers, including water and sewage systems, basements and crawl spaces, drainage and flood risk indicators. That makes past water problems in a Feasterville Trevose basement something to discuss with your agent early, since the form is the seller's statement and not a warranty.

Steps if you are selling after a water loss

  1. Fix the water source and document the repair.
  2. Dry the structure and keep the moisture readings.
  3. Have the affected area cleaned or remediated following S520.
  4. Ask for independent clearance testing if a buyer or lender is likely to ask.
  5. Keep the receipts, photos and reports in one folder.
  6. Talk to your agent about how to describe the history on the disclosure form.

A tidy file often smooths the sale, because the buyer sees the problem and its resolution together.

Reading a clearance report

A clearance report usually lists where samples were taken, what method was used and how results compare with an outdoor or unaffected reference. It should name the standard or protocol that guided the work.

If something looks unclear, ask the tester to explain the terms in plain language. A report you cannot read does not help a buyer, a lender or you. Keep the report with the remediation scope and the moisture readings so the whole story is in one place.

Timing a sale around cleanup

Cleanup, drying and testing take time, and rushing them risks a failed result. Build the schedule so the structure is dry before cleaning, cleaning is finished before testing and testing is finished before listing or closing.

If a buyer asks for testing late in the process, a prompt independent tester and a clear file of earlier work can keep the deal moving.

Common reasons a result comes back poor

The most common reason is that moisture is still present. Materials that look dry on the surface may still be damp inside. The second is cleaning that missed an area, especially in wall cavities and above ceilings.

A third is disturbance, such as dust stirred by work done just before sampling. Planning the sequence, drying first and cleaning thoroughly before testing, reduces the chance of a repeat visit.

If a result is poor, the usual response is to find the missed moisture or area, clean again and retest.

Safety steps while testing and cleanup are underway

Active cleanup areas should stay closed. Keep people and pets out of containment until the crew says the work is finished.

  1. Do not open sealed plastic barriers.
  2. Keep fans from blowing toward the work area.
  3. Wait for the final result before moving belongings back.

Steps if you are buying

Ask for the seller's disclosure and any repair records. If the inspector finds moisture or staining, ask for readings and the history. A dry reading and a documented fix are reassuring, and a high reading is a reason to learn the source before closing.

Questions from Feasterville Trevose homeowners

Does a clearance test guarantee mold will not return?

No. It checks the condition on the day it was done. Mold needs moisture, so the water source must stay fixed.

Is clearance testing required for a home sale in Pennsylvania?

The seller disclosure law requires disclosure of known material defects, and testing is a separate choice. Ask your agent or attorney what applies.

Who pays for clearance testing in a Feasterville Trevose sale?

It is negotiable between buyer and seller. Your agent can advise.

Can the remediation company do the clearance test?

It is better to use an independent tester, so the cleaner is not grading its own work.
